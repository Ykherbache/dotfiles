# Chapter 12 — Planning: Managing Scope

A plan out of touch with reality betrays an unclear relationship. A mutually agreed-upon plan, adjusted to reflect reality, suggests a respectful one.

## The Grocery Shopping Model
Stories = items on shelves. Estimates = prices. Budget = available time. If cart exceeds $100, put something back. Never change estimates or budget during negotiation — change scope.

## Scope as the Control Variable
Time and cost are usually fixed externally. Lowering quality doesn't eliminate work, just shifts it later. Scope is the safe lever: make it explicit, negotiate it.

## Four-Step Planning (any timescale)
1. List items of work
2. Estimate them
3. Set the budget
4. Agree on work within budget

## Estimation
- Estimate in pair-hours: how long for a reasonable pair to complete the story (including testing, refactoring, communication)
- **Yesterday's Weather**: plan this week for what you actually accomplished last week
- First-edition used abstract "points"; Beck now prefers real time for transparency
- Six programmers × 4 hours/day = 12 pair-hours/week
- "Pairing doesn't cut productivity in half — pairs are more than twice as effective"

## When Reality Doesn't Match the Plan
Don't change back to lying. Fix the numbers, communicate consequences. Ask customers to choose priorities. "When things aren't going well is when we most need to adhere to our values."

## Cards on a Wall
Write stories on index cards, post them prominently. Computerized versions never work as well — "you can't automate relationships." Sort cards spatially by risk, value, category. The project manager translates for the rest of the org.

## Key Insight
Plans are not predictions of the future. They express everything you know today about what might happen tomorrow. Their value is in coordination, not prophecy.
