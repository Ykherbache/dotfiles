# Chapter 5 — Principles

Fourteen principles bridge values to practices. Use them to understand practices better and to invent new practices when none fits.

## Humanity
People develop software. Meet human needs: basic safety, accomplishment, belonging, growth, intimacy. Balance individual needs with team needs. Limit work hours to allow rest, exercise, socialization.

## Economics
Time value of money (earn sooner, spend later) + option value (systems and teams that can be redeployed are more valuable). Incremental design defers investment; pay-per-use realizes revenue early.

## Mutual Benefit
**The most important and hardest principle.** Every activity should benefit me now, me later, and my customer. Extensive internal documentation violates this (slows me down now for a hypothetical future person). Instead: automated tests (benefit me now and future maintainers), refactoring (satisfaction now, clarity later), coherent naming.

## Self-Similarity
Copy structures that work across scales. Test→code rhythm works at hours (unit tests), weeks (stories → system tests), quarters (themes → stories). Good place to start, but not the only principle.

## Improvement
"Perfect" is a verb, not an adjective. "Best is the enemy of good enough" misses the point — XP is about excellence through improvement, not waiting for perfection. Start now, improve from there.

## Diversity
Teams need varied skills, attitudes, perspectives. Conflict is opportunity, not a problem. Resolve it productively through respect.

## Reflection
Good teams think about how and why they work. Feelings (fear, anger, anxiety) are valid cues. But reflection must follow action — "learning is action reflected."

## Flow
Deliver a steady stream of value, not big chunks. Respond to stress by making batches smaller, not bigger. When flow breaks, resolve the problems and return to it.

## Opportunity
Transform problems into learning opportunities, not just survival. "Can't make accurate long-term plans? Fine — use a quarterly cycle."

## Redundancy
Solve critical problems multiple ways. Defects are addressed by pair programming, CI, sitting together, customer involvement — redundantly. Don't remove redundancy until proven unnecessary in practice.

## Failure
When stuck between options, try them all. A team with a kitchen timer limited design discussions to 15 minutes, then went and implemented. "Fail instead of talk."

## Quality
Not a control variable. Higher quality → faster delivery. Scope is the lever, not quality. People need to do work they're proud of.

## Baby Steps
"What's the least you could do that is recognizably in the right direction?" Small steps can be taken so rapidly they appear as leaps. Overhead of small steps < cost of recoiling from aborted big changes.

## Accepted Responsibility
Responsibility can only be accepted, never assigned. Whoever signs up for work estimates it. Authority must align with responsibility — misalignment distorts communication.
